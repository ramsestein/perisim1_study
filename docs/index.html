Summary

Abstract

Objective A hospital that receives a prediction model developed elsewhere can adopt and recalibrate it, update it with its own data, or build its own. We asked whether building pays off with the number of patients a hospital has, when, how the hospital can decide from its own sample, and whether the answer holds in single hospitals, over time and when the external model's inputs are not well harmonized.

Methods Four public ICU databases from Korea and the United States (INSPIRE, MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD and NWICU) and four outcomes: hypotension within 6 h, length of stay after ICU discharge and death (T2), vasopressor start within 6 h and ICU discharge within 24 h. A LightGBM developed in other systems was transported to each destination. With 100 to 4,000 local patients (8,000 for T2), in repeated samples, we compared adopting it (logistic recalibration), updating it (boosting from its logit) and building an own model, with tuned variants, and four ways of choosing from the sample. Hypotension and T2 are exploratory. The first confirmatory phase (OSF 10.17605/OSF.IO/YPBVH) used two new outcomes in 9 units. The second (OSF 10.17605/OSF.IO/SAJEX) used 15 single eICU-CRD hospitals, a temporal split of MIMIC-IV and four levels of harmonization error, with balanced options.

Results In the exploratory phase and the first confirmatory phase, where update and build were not balanced, the primary own model did not beat the updated external model in 15 of 17 units, and updating beat adopting from 250 to 1,000 patients in all 9 pre-registered units. Re-run with balanced options in all 17 units (exploratory), building beat updating in 4: the two T2 destinations, and 2 rotation units with 4,000 patients by 0.002 nats or less. In phase 1, H1 held as registered; H2 (cross-validation regret above 0.002 nats in 3 of 9 units; at most 0.0045) and H3 did not. In phase 2 all four hypotheses held. Building did not beat updating in 30 single eICU-CRD hospitals with up to 500 patients nor in the temporal split of MIMIC-IV up to 4,000 (G1). Pooling all scenarios, the share of samples in which the best own model won was 0.37 higher among those flagged by the diagnostic (95 % CI 0.23 to 0.51; G2); without induced harmonization errors the difference was 0.10 (−0.01 to 0.20; exploratory). With harmonization errors, the best own option won in 81 % to 90 % of samples, against 24 % without (G3). With a less demanding criterion than H2, cross-validation regret with 1,000 or more patients had a median of 0.0001 nats and a 90th percentile of 0.0011 (G4).

Conclusion For a hospital with up to a few thousand labelled patients, starting from the external model, recalibrating it with few patients and updating it with more, was the best option or within thousandths of a nat of it; with balanced options, building and updating were within 0.002 nats with 4,000 patients. In single hospitals with up to 500 patients, recalibration was often enough. A harmonization error in the inputs outweighed every other difference and should be corrected before choosing. Outside those errors, the calibration slope and the expected AUROC did not indicate when building pays off. Choosing by cross-validation in the hospital's own sample cost thousandths of a nat.

01Introduction

More and more hospitals receive prediction models developed elsewhere: a research group publishes them, a vendor distributes them or a consortium shares them. In a new hospital the model performs worse, and calibration usually fails first; the ranking of patients holds up better than the level of risk, over time (Davis et al., 2017) and across ICU systems (Yao et al., 2026). Validating the model where it will be used, and repeating that validation over its life, is therefore recommended (Sperrin et al., 2022; Youssef et al., 2023).

After validation the receiving hospital has three options: adopt the model and recalibrate it, update it by re-estimating part or all of it, or build its own. For regression models the updating hierarchy orders the first two from least to most flexible (Janssen et al., 2008; Binuya et al., 2022), Vergouwe et al. (2017) proposed a closed test to pick the step from destination data, and Riley et al. gave sample-size rules for developing and for validating models (Riley et al., 2020, 2021).

For machine-learning models guidance is scarce (Binuya et al., 2022). Multicentre ICU studies show that a local model needs thousands of patients to match one developed elsewhere (Johnson et al., 2018; Bohlen et al., 2026), and that retraining a source model usually beats training from scratch (Tranchellini et al., 2026). In simulations, the type of drift determines the type of update needed (Davis et al., 2019). PRAM mixes a frozen model with neighbours from a local bank and improves on the unadapted model (Jeong et al., 2026); that route without retraining is analysed in a separate paper (Cayuela et al., 2026). No study has tested in a pre-registered way, on new outcomes, when building pays off over starting from the external model, nor with which procedure the hospital should choose.

The framework of Debray et al. (2015) suggests what it depends on. If the destination differs mainly in the level of risk, the external model ranks patients well and correcting its level, or refining it with local data, should suffice. If the predictor–outcome relation changes, no recalibration fixes it, and an own model could pay off early. A second gap is that comparisons are usually made between whole databases extracted and harmonized by one team, whereas a real hospital is a single centre, its patients change over time, and a published or commercial model rarely receives its inputs exactly as it expects them.

This study comes from RECAL, a framework that audits and recalibrates frozen models and ends with a deploy-or-retrain verdict (Marrero García, 2026a, 2026b). We asked four questions: whether building beats starting from the external model with the number of patients a hospital has; whether the type of shift, measured on a few hundred patients, anticipates when building pays off; which procedure lets the hospital choose from its own sample; and whether the answers hold in single hospitals, over time and with poorly harmonized inputs.

02Methods

The study follows TRIPOD+AI (Collins et al., 2024). It is a retrospective transportability study across four public ICU databases with an exploratory phase and two pre-registered confirmatory phases.

Data, outcomes and units

Credentialed PhysioNet databases: INSPIRE (Seoul), MIMIC-IV (Boston), eICU-CRD (208 US hospitals) and NWICU (Chicago); adult post-operative ICU stays. Hypotension and the two new outcomes use landmarks every 2 h up to 48 h after admission, with inputs known at that time (mean arterial pressure features, heart rate, vasopressors, sedation, ventilation, laboratory values, surgery type, hours since admission). Outcomes: hypotension, hourly median MAP below 65 mmHg within 6 h; T2, ordinal (death within 30 days; short, medium or long stay after ICU among survivors); vasopressor start (O1) within 6 h among landmarks without a running vasopressor; ICU discharge (O2) within 24 h. Each combination of external model and destination is a unit.

The options and the sizes

With n labelled local patients the hospital can adopt (intercept-and-slope logistic recalibration of the external logit), update (a LightGBM trained on local labels starting from the external logit) or build (a LightGBM from scratch; variants: tuned LightGBM and L2 logistic regression). In the exploratory phase and phase 1, update and build used fixed settings with 20 % of the local patients held out for early stopping, and only build had tuned variants. n = 250 to 4,000 patients (to 1,000 in NWICU; to 8,000 in T2), drawn within each of 5 patient folds. The primary metric is log-loss in nats, with AUROC and net benefit at thresholds fixed in advance. A break-even is the smallest n at which an option wins in at least 80 % of simulated hospitals.

Diagnosis and choice

On each sample, two diagnostics of the external model: calibration slope, and expected (case-mix) minus observed AUROC (Vergouwe et al., 2010). Slope below 0.8 or a gap above 0.03 flags a relation shift; otherwise a level shift. These thresholds were set after seeing hypotension and T2. Four procedures choose from the sample: patient-grouped cross-validation; Vergouwe's closed test extended to non-nested steps; and two fixed rules (v1, v2).

Phase 1 hypotheses (OSF YPBVH)

H1: in every relation-shift unit the own model beats adopt by 2,000 patients; in every level-shift unit it does not beat update up to 4,000. H2: with 1,000 or more patients, cross-validation has mean regret at most 0.002 nats in every unit. H3: with 1,000 or more patients, rule v2 is within 0.002 nats of cross-validation in every unit. One deviation was documented before rerunning: in one unit the Newton fit of the logistic recalibration diverged; it was replaced by the same likelihood with step-halving and all nine units were rerun, with the same verdicts.

Phase 2: single hospitals, time and harmonization (OSF SAJEX)

The second pre-registration answered four limitations raised in an internal review: simulated hospitals were samples of one database, harmonization was ideal, the options were not balanced, and the diagnostic had been tested only by unit. Three sets of units:

  • H, single hospitals. The 15 eICU-CRD hospitals with at least 600 patients, for hypotension and ICU discharge (30 units), with 100, 250 and 500 local patients.
  • T, time. MIMIC-IV split by year: 2008–2016 as local data, 2017–2022 as test, for the three outcomes (3 units), with 250 to 4,000 patients.
  • M, harmonization errors. The 8 rotation units, with the external model fed inputs that differ from what it expects in four cumulative levels: M0 none; M1 units (lactate, creatinine, haemoglobin, temperature); M2 definitions and unrecorded inputs; M3 coding. The errors touch only what the external model sees; the hospital's own data stay as recorded.

Balanced options. Update and build choose their boosting iterations by inner 3-fold cross-validation and are refitted on all n patients; a tuned update was added. The best external option is the best of adopt, update and tuned update; the best own option, the best of build, tuned build and logistic regression; both are upper bounds. Hypotheses ("wins" = lower log-loss in at least 80 % of samples): G1, in H and T building does not beat updating at any size; G2, pooling every sample, the share in which the best own option beats the best external one is higher in flagged samples (unit-clustered bootstrap risk difference excluding 0); G3, in M that share rises from M0 to M3 (one-sided stratified trend test) and, in M3 with 4,000 patients, building beats updating in at least half of the 8 units; G4, in T and M cells with at least 1,000 patients, cross-validation regret has a median ≤ 0.002 and a 90th percentile ≤ 0.005 nats, and does not exceed the closed test's in at least 75 % of cells. G4 is a less demanding re-test of H2, registered after H2 had failed.

03Results

Starting from the external model was the best option, or within thousandths of a nat of it, in almost every setting. Building won clearly where the predictor–outcome relation had changed and where the external model received broken inputs.

All 17 units with balanced options

Re-run with the balanced options of phase 2 and no induced error (an exploratory sensitivity analysis), building beat updating in 4 of the 17 units: in both T2 destinations, from 1,000 (MIMIC-IV) and 250 patients (eICU-CRD), by 0.012 to 0.025 nats up to 4,000 patients; and in 2 rotation units with 4,000 patients, by 0.0003 nats (vasopressor start in eICU-CRD) and 0.002 nats (ICU discharge in INSPIRE). In the 7 NWICU units it did not, up to 1,000 patients.

Secondary analyses

In a semi-synthetic map with controlled relation shift, updating beat building with 250 to 1,000 patients in all but 2 combinations. In hypotension, the recalibrated model stayed miscalibrated in landmarks with a running vasopressor in MIMIC-IV (+0.233) and eICU-CRD (−0.311); no miscalibration of that size was seen in that stratum in NWICU. A LightGBM with only the mean arterial pressure features reached 94 % to 103 % of the full model's gain: hypotension is close to autoregressive.

    How to compute the calibration slope and the expected AUROC

    With p the external model's predicted risks on your labelled patients and y their outcomes (0/1): fit the slope by logistic regression of y on logit(p); for the expected AUROC, recalibrate the intercept, simulate outcomes from those risks many times and average the AUROC (Vergouwe et al., 2010). The gap is expected minus observed.

    import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

def shift_diagnosis(p, y, n_sim=200, seed=0):
    p = np.clip(np.asarray(p, float), 1e-6, 1 - 1e-6); y = np.asarray(y, int)
    lp = np.log(p / (1 - p)).reshape(-1, 1)
    slope = LogisticRegression(C=1e6).fit(lp, y).coef_[0, 0]
    # intercept-only recalibration: offset logit(p) by a common shift
    a = 0.0
    for _ in range(50):
        mu = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(a + lp[:, 0]))); a += (y - mu).sum() / (mu * (1 - mu)).sum()
    q = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(a + lp[:, 0])))
    g = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    exp_auc = np.mean([roc_auc_score(g.random(q.size) < q, q) for _ in range(n_sim)])
    obs_auc = roc_auc_score(y, p)
    return {"slope": slope, "auroc_observed": obs_auc, "auroc_expected": exp_auc,
            "relation_shift": slope < 0.8 or exp_auc - obs_auc > 0.03}

    04Discussion

    For a hospital that receives an external model and has up to a few thousand labelled patients, starting from that model was the best option or within thousandths of a nat of it, and a few hundred patients sufficed to recalibrate it. With unbalanced options, building did not beat updating in 15 of 17 units; with balanced options it began to win in 2 of 8 units near 4,000 patients, by 0.002 nats or less, and stayed behind in the rest. In 30 single eICU-CRD hospitals with up to 500 patients, building never beat updating, but updating beat recalibrating in only 12: with so few patients, recalibration was often enough. Building paid off clearly when the predictor–outcome relation changed and when the external model received poorly harmonized inputs — a case in which the right move is to fix the input.

    When the destination differs mainly in the level of risk, the external model already ranks patients well: recalibration corrects its level and updating adds what is specific to the destination without losing what was learned elsewhere, whereas an own model must relearn that ranking from a few hundred or thousand patients. When the relation changes, the external ranking is partly wrong and no monotone recalibration fixes it. When the inputs are wrong, the external score no longer means what it meant, and a model trained on the hospital's correctly recorded data wins — expected by construction; the harmonization set measures how much.

    What changed after the internal reviews
    • The headline softened. "Building never wins up to 4,000" came from unbalanced options. Re-run balanced in all 17 units, building won in both T2 destinations (relation shift) and in 2 rotation units near 4,000 patients, by thousandths of a nat; not in NWICU.
    • Wins with a margin. Counting a win only above 0.002 nats, own-model wins in single hospitals fell from 15 % to 12 % of samples, and without induced errors the diagnostic still did not separate them (risk difference 0.07, 95 % CI −0.02 to 0.15).
    • The diagnostic does not tell when to build. G2 held because induced errors break the external model and a slope below 0.8 detects that almost by construction. Without them, flagged and unflagged samples barely differ; in single hospitals the NPV (0.86) is the base rate (0.85).
    • G4 is a weaker re-test of H2, registered after H2 failed, and held with balanced options.
    • G1 had little room to fail: 500 patients at most in single hospitals, 3 units over time.
    • Harmonization errors call for a fix, not a new model. Build only when the input cannot be corrected (for example, a closed vendor model).

    An operational guide for the receiving hospital

    Check the inputsVerify that the external model receives its inputs with the units, definitions and coding it expects. If not, fix the mapping and evaluate again; build an own model only if the input cannot be corrected.
    RecalibrateAdopt the external model and recalibrate it (intercept, or intercept and slope). 250 patients sufficed; in single hospitals with up to 500 patients this was often enough.
    UpdateWith more labels, update the external model with local data rather than replacing it. Updating beat recalibration from 250 to 1,000 patients in the pre-registered units; balanced, building came within 0.002 nats only near 4,000.
    Choose by cross-validationCompare the options — an own model among them once you have thousands of patients — by patient-grouped cross-validation in your own sample. Do not decide by fixed size thresholds or by the shift diagnostic.

    Relation to previous work

    That local models need thousands of patients to match models developed elsewhere had been seen in ICU data (Johnson et al., 2018; Bohlen et al., 2026), and retraining a source deep-learning model for sepsis usually beat training from scratch (Tranchellini et al., 2026). Our results confirm this with tree models, across four outcomes and two continents, and add two pre-registered phases, the size of the differences in log-loss and net benefit, single hospitals, time and harmonization errors. Davis et al. (2019) showed in simulations that the type of drift determines the type of update. The closed test of Vergouwe et al. (2017) protects the most parsimonious step and tends to stay at recalibration with small samples (Van Calster et al., 2017; Schnellinger et al., 2021); extended to machine-learning options, it had more regret than cross-validation in most settings. Riley's sample-size rules answer a different question and did not help decide against an available external model.

    Limitations

    • Few relation-shift settings. The exception rests on three settings, two exploratory; NWICU units are not independent of the rotation and reach only 1,000 patients; single hospitals, only 500.
    • Refuted and low-power hypotheses. H2 failed by small margins and H3 clearly; G4, which replaces H2, is less demanding; G1 had little room to fail. Diagnostic thresholds were set on the exploratory data.
    • Asymmetric options in the first part. Figure 2, Figure 3 and the unit table rest on unbalanced options; the balanced re-run of all 17 units (Figure 4) is exploratory and post hoc. In phase 2 the best option of each kind was chosen on the test set; the own variants are more diverse than the external ones, so their maximum is more optimistic.
    • Wins at any margin. The registered per-sample wins count any log-loss difference. Re-read with a 0.002-nat margin (exploratory), the conclusions were unchanged.
    • Simulated harmonization errors, defined in advance and plausible, but not those of a real integration; outside set M, external and local data shared extraction, definitions and units, which favours starting from the external model.
    • One family of external models, all LightGBM; with deep-learning models break-evens may differ. Public databases: part of the shift is in how systems record data.
    • Outcome-specific caveats: hypotension is close to autoregressive (Enevoldsen & Vistisen, 2022), vasopressor start is rare (2 % to 3 %), and stay and discharge depend on organisational decisions.

    05Conclusion

    For a hospital that receives a prediction model developed elsewhere, the default answer is to start from it, not to replace it. Recalibration sufficed with a few hundred patients; updating was better with more patients in the pre-registered units, though in single hospitals with up to 500 patients recalibration was often enough; and an own model came within thousandths of a nat of the updated one up to 4,000 patients, except when the external model ranked patients wrongly. Before deciding, check that the model receives its inputs as it expects them and fix them if not. The calibration slope and the expected AUROC were not enough to know when to build. To choose, cross-validation in the hospital's own sample cost thousandths of a nat at most.
