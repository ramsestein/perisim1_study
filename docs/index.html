Update before you build

When does a hospital gain from its own prediction model instead of an external one? A study across four ICU databases with a pre-registered confirmatory phase.

If you already have an external model, recalibrate it with a few hundred patients, update it with more, and build your own only when the external model ranks your patients wrongly.

Abstract

Objective. A hospital that receives a prediction model developed elsewhere can adopt and recalibrate it, update it with its own data, or build its own. We asked whether building pays off with the number of patients a hospital has, when, and how the hospital can decide from its own sample.

Methods. Four public ICU databases from Korea and the United States (INSPIRE, MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD and NWICU) and four outcomes: hypotension within 6 h, length of stay after ICU discharge and death (T2), vasopressor start within 6 h, and ICU discharge within 24 h. A LightGBM developed in other systems was transported to each destination. With 250 to 4,000 local patients (8,000 for T2), in repeated samples, we compared adopting it (logistic recalibration), updating it (boosting that starts from its logit) and building an own model (LightGBM from scratch; as variants, a tuned LightGBM and an L2 logistic regression). Four ways of choosing from the hospital's sample were evaluated: cross-validation, Vergouwe's closed test and two rules based on two shift diagnostics. The first two outcomes are exploratory; the last two form a pre-registered confirmatory phase (OSF 10.17605/OSF.IO/YPBVH) with three hypotheses.

Results. Across 17 outcome–destination units, the primary own model beat the updated external model only for T2 (2 destinations, by 0.007 to 0.028 nats). In the pre-registered phase (9 units) it beat it in none up to 4,000 patients, and updating beat adopting from 250 to 1,000 patients in all 9. Own-model variants beat the better external option in 3 units: by 0.002 nats or less in two, and by 0.011 to 0.024 nats with 250 to 500 patients in the third. All three settings in which building won by more than 0.002 nats showed a change in the predictor–outcome relation (calibration slope below 0.8 or observed AUROC more than 0.03 below its case-mix expectation); in the pre-registered phase that diagnosis anticipated the winning strategy in all 5 evaluable units (H1 held). Cross-validated choice cost at most 0.0045 nats with 1,000 or more patients, above the pre-registered bound of 0.002 in 3 of 9 units (H2 did not hold). A fixed rule did not match cross-validation (H3 did not hold; 2 of 9), and the closed test had more regret than cross-validation in 28 of 37 unit–size combinations.

Conclusion. For a hospital with up to a few thousand labelled patients, starting from the external model, recalibrating it with few patients and updating it with more, was the best option or within thousandths of a nat of it. Building an own model paid off only when the external model ranked patients wrongly, which a few hundred patients allow one to suspect. To choose, cross-validation in the hospital's own sample came closer to the best option than fixed rules or the closed test.

1Introduction

More and more hospitals receive prediction models developed elsewhere: a research group publishes them, a vendor distributes them or a consortium shares them. In a new hospital the model performs worse, and calibration usually fails first; the ranking of patients holds up better than the level of risk, over time (Davis et al., 2017) and across ICU systems (Yao et al., 2026). Validating the model where it will be used, and repeating that validation over its life, is therefore recommended (Sperrin et al., 2022; Youssef et al., 2023).

After validation the receiving hospital has three options: adopt the model and recalibrate it, update it by re-estimating part or all of it, or build its own. For regression models the updating hierarchy orders the first two from least to most flexible (Janssen et al., 2008; Binuya et al., 2022), Vergouwe et al. (2017) proposed a closed test to pick the step from destination data, and Riley et al. gave sample-size rules for developing and for validating models (Riley et al., 2020, 2021).

For machine-learning models guidance is scarce (Binuya et al., 2022). Multicentre ICU studies show that a local model needs thousands of patients to match one developed elsewhere (Johnson et al., 2018; Bohlen et al., 2026), and that retraining a source model usually beats training from scratch (Tranchellini et al., 2026). In simulations, the type of drift determines the type of update needed (Davis et al., 2019). PRAM mixes a frozen model with neighbours from a local bank and improves on the unadapted model (Jeong et al., 2026). No study has tested in a pre-registered way, on new outcomes, when building an own model pays off against starting from the external one, nor how the hospital should choose.

The framework of Debray et al. (2015) suggests what it depends on. If the destination differs mainly in the level of risk, the external model ranks patients well and correcting its level, or refining it with local data, should suffice. If the predictor–outcome relation changes, no recalibration fixes it, and an own model could pay off early. This study comes from RECAL, a framework that audits and recalibrates frozen models and ends with a deploy-or-retrain verdict (Marrero García, 2026a, 2026b); here we put that verdict to the test. We asked three questions: whether building beats starting from the external model with the patients a hospital has, and whether this holds on new outcomes; whether the type of shift, measured on a few hundred patients, anticipates when building pays off; and which procedure a hospital should use to choose from its own sample.

2Methods

The study follows TRIPOD+AI (Collins et al., 2024). It is a retrospective transportability study across four public ICU databases with an exploratory phase (hypotension, T2) and a pre-registered confirmatory phase (vasopressor start, ICU discharge). The exploratory analyses were specified in writing before they ran, but after the transport results of those outcomes were known; the diagnostic thresholds were set from them. The confirmatory phase was registered on OSF before any model was fitted for its two new outcomes.

Data, outcomes and units

Credentialed PhysioNet databases: INSPIRE (Seoul), MIMIC-IV (Boston), eICU-CRD (208 US hospitals) and NWICU (Chicago); adult post-operative ICU stays. Hypotension and the two new outcomes use landmarks every 2 h up to 48 h after admission, with inputs known at that time (mean arterial pressure features, heart rate, vasopressors, sedation, ventilation, laboratory values, surgery type, hours since admission). Outcomes: hypotension, hourly median MAP below 65 mmHg within 6 h; T2, ordinal (death within 30 days; short, medium or long stay after ICU among survivors); vasopressor start (O1) within 6 h among landmarks off vasopressors (INSPIRE records no infusions); ICU discharge (O2) within 24 h. Each pair of external model and destination is a unit: 6 for hypotension, 2 for T2 and 9 in the confirmatory phase.

The options and the sizes

With n labelled local patients the hospital can adopt (intercept-and-slope logistic recalibration of the external logit), update (a LightGBM trained on local labels starting from the external logit) or build (a LightGBM from scratch; fixed settings; variants: tuned LightGBM and L2 logistic regression). n = 250 to 4,000 patients (to 1,000 in NWICU; to 8,000 in T2), drawn within each of 5 patient folds (5 draws up to 1,000, 3 above) and scored on the fold's test landmarks. The primary metric is log-loss in nats, with AUROC and net benefit at thresholds fixed in advance. A break-even is the smallest n at which an option wins in at least 80 % of simulated hospitals.

Diagnosis and choice

On each sample, two diagnostics of the external model: calibration slope, and expected (case-mix) minus observed AUROC (Vergouwe et al., 2010). Slope below 0.8 or a gap above 0.03 flags a relation shift; otherwise a level shift. Four procedures choose from the sample: 3-fold patient-grouped cross-validation; Vergouwe's closed test extended to non-nested steps (keep, intercept, logistic, then update, then build; one-sided z on per-patient log-loss sums, α = 0.05); rule v1 (relation shift: update from 500 patients; otherwise adopt); and rule v2, fixed before the confirmatory phase (relation shift: build from 1,000; level shift: adopt, update from 4,000). Regret is the log-loss of the choice minus the best of adopt, update and build. Riley et al. (2020) minimum sizes were computed beside the break-evens.

Pre-registered hypotheses

H1: in every relation-shift unit the own model beats adopt by 2,000 patients; in every level-shift unit it does not beat update up to 4,000. H2: with 1,000 or more patients, cross-validation has mean regret at most 0.002 nats in every unit. H3: with 1,000 or more patients, rule v2 is within 0.002 nats of cross-validation in every unit. One deviation was documented before rerunning: in one unit the Newton fit of the logistic recalibration diverged; it was replaced by the same likelihood with step-halving (identical whenever Newton converges) and all nine units were rerun; verdicts did not change.

3Results

The diagnosis flagged a relation shift in vasopressor start in MIMIC-IV (97 % of samples with 250 to 1,000 patients) and in ICU discharge in INSPIRE (75 %), and a level shift in the other seven (0 % to 29 %). The NWICU units, which reach only 1,000 patients, could not decide H1. H2 failed in ICU discharge in eICU-CRD (0.0021 nats with 1,000 patients) and in two NWICU units (0.0028 and 0.0045). H3 failed in seven units: with 1,000 and 2,000 patients rule v2 adopted in level-shift units where updating was better, with an excess regret of 0.0046 to 0.0332 nats over cross-validation.

All units at a glance

Smallest number of local patients at which the first option wins in at least 80 % of simulated hospitals; "never" up to the largest size evaluated.

Secondary analyses

In a semi-synthetic map with controlled relation shift, updating beat building with 250 to 1,000 patients in all but 2 combinations. A similarity-based alternative, a kNN with a metric learned at source, transported as pre-registered to NWICU (retention ratio 1.41 to 1.53), but the recalibrated external model with 500 patients beat it in every unit. A replica of PRAM improved its unadapted base model, yet logistic recalibration of that same base beat it in 11 of 12 combinations. In hypotension, the recalibrated model stayed miscalibrated in landmarks with a running vasopressor in MIMIC-IV (calibration-in-the-large +0.233) and eICU-CRD (−0.311), but not in NWICU.

4Discussion

For a hospital that receives an external model and has up to a few thousand labelled patients, starting from that model was the best option or close to it in 14 of 17 settings, including 8 of the 9 pre-registered ones. A few hundred patients sufficed to recalibrate it; from 250 to 1,000, updating it with local data beat recalibration in all pre-registered units. Building paid off clearly in three settings, all with a change in the predictor–outcome relation.

When the destination differs mainly in the level of risk, the external model already ranks patients well: recalibration corrects its level and updating adds what is specific to the destination without losing what was learned elsewhere, whereas an own model must relearn that ranking from a few hundred or thousand patients. When the relation changes, the external ranking is partly wrong and no monotone recalibration fixes it; starting from it then becomes a burden even for updating. A relation shift is necessary but not sufficient: in vasopressor start in MIMIC-IV the diagnosis flagged it and building won by less than 0.002 nats. The diagnosis tells when recalibration is not enough and when an own model is worth trying, not how much it will gain.

An operational guide for the receiving hospital

RecalibrateAdopt the external model and recalibrate it (intercept, or intercept and slope). In this study, 250 patients sufficed to calibrate it.
UpdateWith more labels, update the external model with local data rather than replacing it. Updating beat recalibration from 250 to 1,000 patients in the pre-registered units.
DiagnoseMeasure the calibration slope and the expected versus observed AUROC; with a slope below 0.8 or an observed AUROC more than 0.03 below expectation, also try an own model.
ChooseCompare the options by patient-grouped cross-validation in your own sample, not by fixed size thresholds.

Relation to previous work

That local models need thousands of patients to match models developed elsewhere had been seen in ICU data (Johnson et al., 2018; Bohlen et al., 2026), and retraining a source deep-learning model for sepsis usually beat training from scratch (Tranchellini et al., 2026). Our results confirm this with tree models, across four outcomes and two continents, and add a pre-registered phase on new outcomes, the size of the difference in log-loss and net benefit, and the delimitation of the exception. Davis et al. (2019) showed in simulations that the type of drift determines the type of update; we observed it in real data and tested it prospectively. The closed test was known to favour recalibration with small samples (Van Calster et al., 2017; Schnellinger et al., 2021); extended to machine-learning options, it had more regret than cross-validation in most settings, above all where building was best. Riley's rules answer a different question and should not be used to decide against an available external model.

Limitations

  • Few relation-shift settings. The exception rests on three settings, two exploratory, and H1 on five evaluable units; NWICU units are not independent of the rotation and reach only 1,000 patients.
  • Refuted hypotheses. H2 failed by small margins (up to 0.0045 nats) and H3 clearly; diagnostic thresholds were set on the exploratory data.
  • Asymmetric options. Updating used fixed settings while one own-model variant was tuned; tuning the update would probably reduce that variant's small wins.
  • One family of external models, all LightGBM; with deep-learning models break-evens may differ.
  • Public databases and recording differences: part of the shift is in how systems record data (INSPIRE has no running infusions; lactate missingness varies).
  • Outcome-specific caveats: hypotension is partly autoregressive (Enevoldsen & Vistisen, 2022), vasopressor start is rare (2 % to 3 %), and stay and discharge depend on organisational decisions.

5Conclusion

For a hospital that receives a prediction model developed elsewhere, the default answer is to update it, not to replace it. Recalibration sufficed with a few hundred patients, updating was better from about 250 to 1,000, and building an own model paid off only when the external model ranked patients wrongly, a change that two diagnostics measured on a few hundred patients allow one to suspect. To choose, cross-validation in the hospital's own sample came closer to the best option than any fixed rule, though not always within 0.002 nats.
